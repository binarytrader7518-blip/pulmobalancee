Mullein Leaf: What Tradition and Research Say

Why a roadside weed became one of the best-known herbs for the throat and chest, and what science has actually tested.

By the Pulmo Balance editorial team · Updated November 2026 · Educational content, not medical advice

Mullein (Verbascum thapsus) is hard to miss: a tall spike of yellow flowers rising from a rosette of soft, felt-like leaves. It grows along roadsides and in meadows across Europe, Asia and North America, and it has been part of herbal practice for centuries. Today it's one of the most familiar ingredients in respiratory-wellness formulas, including Pulmo Balance.

A long traditional history

Herbal traditions have used mullein leaves and flowers, mostly as teas and infusions, for the throat and chest. Its popularity in modern supplements comes mainly from that long track record.

What researchers have studied

Scientific reviews of mullein list a wide range of plant compounds, including flavonoids, iridoid glycosides, saponins and phenylethanoid glycosides. Laboratory studies have explored antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity. Two useful starting points are Health-promoting and disease-mitigating potential of Verbascum thapsus L. (common mullein): A review (National Library of Medicine (PubMed)) and Common mullein (Verbascum thapsus L.): recent advances in research (National Library of Medicine (PubMed)).

Keep it in perspective: most mullein research is experimental, carried out in the lab or in animals. Well-designed human trials are scarce, so mullein's role rests largely on traditional use rather than proven clinical effects.

Using mullein sensibly

  • Choose products from sellers that publish clear directions and a contact address.
  • Tell your doctor or pharmacist about any herbal products you take.
  • Never use an herb in place of prescribed respiratory medicine.
